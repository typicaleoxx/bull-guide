topic: Studying and surviving an Operating Systems course

Operating Systems is widely considered one of the hardest required courses in a CS degree. It covers process management, scheduling algorithms, memory management, virtual memory, file systems, and concurrency. Students who walk in expecting it to be a programming course similar to Data Structures are often surprised by how much theory is involved alongside the implementation work.

Concurrency is the topic that causes the most difficulty. Understanding race conditions, mutex locks, semaphores, and deadlock is hard because bugs do not appear consistently. A program with a race condition might run correctly nine out of ten times, making it easy to miss in testing. Practicing with simple concurrent programs that deliberately trigger race conditions helps build the instincts needed to catch them.

Projects in OS courses are typically long. Implementing a basic shell, a memory allocator, or parts of a file system can each take 15 to 25 hours for students who are working steadily. Starting at least a week before the deadline is not optional advice in this class.

Reading the textbook matters more in this course than in most others. Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces is a commonly used text that explains concepts clearly with diagrams and pseudocode. Relying only on slides tends to leave gaps that appear on exams.

Students who performed well consistently mentioned keeping a personal notes file where they summarized each major concept in their own words. Returning to those summaries before exams was faster and more effective than re-reading entire chapters.
