topic: Learning Boolean logic, circuits, and digital design

Computer Logic, sometimes called Digital Logic or Logic Design, introduces how hardware works at a low level. The course covers Boolean algebra, logic gates, truth tables, Karnaugh maps, combinational circuits, and often sequential circuits like flip-flops and state machines. Students who plan to study computer architecture or embedded systems find this course especially relevant.

The biggest early challenge is connecting algebra to physical circuits. Knowing that AND, OR, and NOT gates correspond to multiplication, addition, and complement in Boolean algebra is a starting point, but students need practice building and simplifying circuits before that connection feels automatic. Karnaugh maps are a tool that many students underestimate until exam time.

Lab sections vary by school, but most require students to build and test circuits using simulation software or physical breadboards. Paying attention during lab, even when it feels repetitive, helps reinforce concepts that can seem abstract from lecture alone.

Sequential circuits tend to be the hardest part for most students. State machine diagrams require you to think about behavior over time, which is a different skill from the combinational logic covered earlier in the course. Drawing clean state diagrams and converting them to truth tables is worth practicing well before the relevant exam.

Students recommend forming a group specifically for homework problems in this course. Many circuit problems have multiple valid solutions, and comparing your design with a classmate often reveals simpler approaches you would not find by working alone.
